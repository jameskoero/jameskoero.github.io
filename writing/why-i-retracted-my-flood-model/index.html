Why I retracted my own flood model

A near-perfect score, two flood rows, and what I changed

Case studiesResearchWritingExperienceSkillsAboutCV

Last updated: 18 Nov 2026

My first flood model for the Nyando basin reported an AUC of 0.9717, an F1 of 0.9022 and a cross-validated AUC of 0.9727. Those numbers looked excellent. They were wrong, and I retracted them.

What was wrong

The training file had 2,308 rows. Only 2 of them were labelled as flooded. To balance the classes, SMOTE created synthetic flood rows by interpolating between real ones, and it ran before the data was split into training and test sets.

So the test set contained near-copies of points the model had already seen. With only two real flood rows, every synthetic flood row lay on the line between those two points. The model was not learning what floods look like. It was recognising variations of two rows. The score measured memory, not skill.

How I found it

A leakage audit of the first version asked a plain question of the headline number: what exactly was tested, and on which rows? The answer was in the training file itself. No clever diagnostic was needed, only the habit of looking at the data before trusting the metric.

What I did

  • Retracted the figures in the repository README, with a correction notice at the top.
  • Added an erratum to both earlier Zenodo records on 3 November 2026.
  • Rebuilt the data from scratch: 4,420 rows from 35 Sentinel-1 scene dates, with flood labels from Copernicus GFM, an independent source.
  • Changed how the model is tested: leave-one-event-out validation, decision rules written before any score, and 27 sealed flood dates scored once.

What the honest number looks like

The rebuilt model scores a mean per-event AUC of 0.943 on 23 unseen flood events, and 0.971 on the 27 sealed events. The first is lower than 0.9717 and the second is close to it, but both now mean something: each was earned on floods the model never saw. As a check against circularity, a bare four-feature logistic regression scores 0.841, so the data is not trivially easy.

What I took from it

  • Resample only inside the training fold, never before the split.
  • Count the positive labels before reading any metric.
  • A result that is too good is a reason to look harder, not to publish faster.
  • Publishing a correction costs less than defending a number that is not true.

The full evaluation design is on the model evaluation page, and the code and records are in the public repository.
